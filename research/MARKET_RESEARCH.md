# Lame Brain Ideas — Market research and feasibility

**Research date:** 2026-10-07
**Geography:** United States; first operating market still to be selected
**Method:** authoritative secondary research plus a bottom-up operating hypothesis; no commissioned primary study

## Finding

The concept merits a bounded paid pilot, not a flagship-first launch. The strongest wedge is a human-guided, outcome-based service for people and organizations who need help turning uncertainty into a decision, evidence, and a practical artifact. The market has many adjacent substitutes—self-service AI, SCORE and SBDCs, consultants, agencies, maker spaces, accelerators, and professional specialists—so “help with ideas” is not enough. The company must win on a clearly defined outcome, trust, speed, follow-through, and appropriate referrals.

## Market definition

The useful market is not everyone who has an idea. It is the subset with:

1. a real decision within a defined time window;
2. enough authority and budget to act;
3. a problem that can be advanced through facilitation, research, planning, and an artifact;
4. no requirement for unlicensed or unsafe advice; and
5. a reachable trusted channel.

The first obtainable market is constrained by delivery hours and partner distribution. National market-share arithmetic would create false precision.

## Competitive structure

| Alternative | Strength | Lame Brain response |
|---|---|---|
| Self-service generative AI | Immediate, inexpensive drafting and brainstorming | Sell framing, source quality, synthesis, human judgment, and follow-through—not document volume |
| SCORE / SBDC | Trusted, subsidized mentoring and assistance | Partner and refer; focus on production, facilitation, and completion |
| Freelancers and agencies | Deep specialist craft | Curate a decision journey and vetted bench; never imply one generalist can replace specialists |
| Accelerators/incubators | Cohort, network, education, and sometimes capital | Serve earlier and broader needs without taking equity at launch |
| Maker spaces | Tools, physical prototyping, and community | Partner instead of owning underused equipment |
| Crowdfunding platforms | Existing backer/payment infrastructure | Offer readiness work; do not build a platform in the launch phase |
| Professional firms | Licensed or specialized judgment | Refer when the decision crosses legal, tax, patent, medical, investment, engineering, or safety boundaries |

## Pricing and unit-economics thesis

The launch model tests a weighted average service price of $1,718.75, 8.71 delivery hours per engagement, 8.3% materials/pass-through, 3% processing, and $65/hour delivery labor. At the modeled offer mix, service contribution exceeds 50%. Every one of those commercial inputs remains an assumption until paid cases generate invoices, time logs, direct costs, rework, refunds, and outcomes.

Low-price sessions can acquire learning and customers, but premium bounded work and partner programs must carry the professional operating model. Discounts should be attributed to acquisition or sponsorship rather than silently reducing realized price.

## Distribution thesis

Trusted hosts can reduce the credibility and acquisition problem for a new category. The founding channel experiment should compare:

- host-recruited clinic participants
- direct professional referrals
- founder/customer referrals
- media-driven inquiries
- limited targeted outreach

Every lead needs a source, date, segment, decision, booked status, paid status, offer, and reason won/lost. Broad paid advertising is premature before one message/channel combination converts.

## Media thesis

Podcast and video are credible supporting channels because consumption is broad and dual-format behavior is normal. The editorial strategy should prove method and integrity, not chase reach. Qualified actions—submitted decisions, host invitations, expert corrections, discovery bookings, and paid cases—matter more than views.

YouTube reported more than one billion monthly active viewers of podcast content in 2025, reinforcing the case for video distribution, but that platform statistic still does not predict audience or conversion for Lame Brain Ideas.

## AI, IP, and trust implications

Generative AI strengthens the need for a clear human-accountability position:

- use current task-appropriate models and record the provider/tool actually used;
- validate material claims through sources and specialists;
- document human expressive and analytical contribution where ownership matters;
- do not treat prompting alone as proof of authorship;
- recognize only natural persons as inventors under current USPTO guidance;
- keep customer content out of unapproved systems;
- use the NIST AI RMF and its generative-AI profile as risk-management references.

## Crowdfunding and investor activity

SEC data through June 30, 2026 reports 9,851 Regulation Crowdfunding offerings, 4,517 offerings reporting proceeds, and approximately $1.644 billion in total reported proceeds since May 2016—about $364,000 per offering reporting proceeds. This demonstrates an active regulated market, not a launch opportunity for an unregistered intermediary.

The company may eventually prepare customers for rewards campaigns or refer to qualified intermediaries. The launch case excludes platform fees, investor memberships, securities recommendations, transaction compensation, and equity interests. Any change requires a new legal and investment case.

## Location thesis

Physical presence can make the service legible and welcoming, but it is not yet proven to be an economical moat. The pilot should use partner venues, temporary residencies, and portable recording/workshop equipment. A permanent location requires:

- repeatable paid demand and acquisition by segment
- acceptable contribution and cash case
- demonstrated need that cannot be solved through hosts/digital delivery
- three broker-backed options and contractor estimates
- accessibility, insurance, parking/transit, hours, and partner-density analysis
- a downside case that does not depend on speculative revenue

## Feasibility scorecard

| Dimension | Rating | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Problem relevance | Strong | Many builders struggle to frame, validate, and finish cross-functional work |
| Category legibility | Promising | “Idea studio” can be made tangible through offers and artifacts |
| Paid demand | Unproven | No primary interviews or paid cases in the current record |
| Launch regulatory burden | Manageable with boundaries | Consulting, workshops, and media can launch with controls and referrals |
| Unit economics | Plausible, unvalidated | Model works at assumed mix/hours; paid actuals are absent |
| Capital efficiency | Stronger as portable pilot | $125K 90-day ceiling versus multi-million flagship trajectory |
| Defensibility | Moderate | Brand, outcomes data, workflows, partner network, and trust can compound |
| Scale readiness | Low | Delivery, acquisition, governance, and founder dependence are untested |

## Primary research plan

### Before first spend gate

- 30 problem/decision interviews, including at least 10 existing business owners and 10 first-time builders
- 10 host interviews with libraries, SBDCs, maker spaces, universities, chambers, or coworking operators
- moderated name tests, not just preference polls
- price conversations using concrete scope and tradeoffs

### During paid pilot

- 12 paid cases by Month 3 across at least two offers
- complete time/cost/outcome data for every case
- attributed conversion and loss reasons
- 14-day action follow-up
- three host experiments and eight media pieces

### Decision standard

Proceed only if one segment and at least two offers show a credible path to repeatable acquisition, useful outcomes, and contribution at a delivery load below 85%. Otherwise narrow, redesign, pause, or stop.
